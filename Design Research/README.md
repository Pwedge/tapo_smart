# Overview
This directory contains various captures/scripts/notes/etc that were used to understand the communication between the two microcontrollers used in the S500D.

**NOTE:** The majority of the notes in the files should be ignored, as they were based on early captures where the two UART receive pins were mixed up.  This lead to an inverse understanding of which microcontroller was sending requests and which microcontroller was sending responses.  The raw data captures are accurate, as is the information below and in the ESPHome tapo_smart driver.

# System Design
## Power
The 120V input power is fused via a 4A fuse.  The load output is handled by a T1235H triac that has its gate enabled mid-cycle by a microcontroller based on the desired dimmer level.  The 5V logic voltage for the board is generated by a half-wave rectifier from mains along with a KP3210-based SMPS to provide a maximum of ~400mA of current.  The 5V supply goes through a solid state power switch that is gated by the "Restart" button on the switch.  The 5V supply is further regulated down to 3.3V via an LDO in a RTL8710CF microcontroller.  This supply is generated by supplying the VBAT_IN supply input on the RTL8710CF with 5V, and the 3.3V is provided on the VD33_OUT on the same device.  The 3.3V supply is then used by a handful of other devices on the board, including a second microcontroller.

## Control Logic
The TP-Link Tapo S500D and TP-Link Kasa HS220 contain two microcontrollers that communicate with each other via a serial UART operating at 115200 buad (8-N-1).  The primary microcontroller is an RTL8710CF (RTL8720CF on the Kasa HS220).  This microcontroller handles the 2.4GHz wifi and sends control frames to the other microcontroller to set the dimming level and control the seven LEDs near the "+" and "-" buttons on the top of the switch.

The frames sent by the RTL8710CF are received by a CMS8S5880 microcontroller.  This microcontroller appears to run a very tight loop that simply handles the received messages and sets its outputs to control the row of seven LEDs, as well as watch for zero-crossing in order to handle enabling the triac at the appropriate time based on the desired dimming value.  This microcontroller appears to be very dumb and does not handle interpolation between dimming values; it is the job of the main microcontroller to rapidly send new values to the CMS8S5880 to create the illusion of dimming.

## Thermal
There appears to be a diode in the vecinity of the triac that is used by the CMS8S5880 to determine temperature.  Not much research was done to understand the analog implementation of this.  Instead, the switch was placed in a crude thermal chamber (a 3D printer's chamber).  The chamber temperature was then ramped and the output of the CMS8S5880 was checked to see what the corresponding value was.  This was recorded in the "dimmer vt curve.xlsx" file and was used to generate a first order approximiation of the relationship between the temperature of the triac and the value that is reported by the CMS8S5880.
